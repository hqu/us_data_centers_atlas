Distance and timing analysis · local restrictions vs. data centers

Backlash next door

The fight over data centers started in the towns that already host them. In 2026 it broke out of those hubs: most new restrictions now come from places 50 or more miles from any operating data center, and they usually follow a proposal landing nearby.

The movement started next door and is fanning out

Each shaded circle is the area within 25 miles of an operating data center. A hub is a group of operating data centers chained together so that every site is within 25 miles of at least one other site in the group. A big hub is one with 12 or more operating sites. Proposals come first, then restrictions

Orange diamonds are proposed data center projects, shown from the month FracTracker first logged them. Restrictions follow. When a restriction comes after a proposal within 25 miles, an orange line connects them.

Local restrictions are mostly reactions to specific projects. A developer files for a rezoning or buys farmland, neighbors organize, and within months the town or county pauses or limits data centers. The project nearby, not the data centers already running in the region, is what tips a community into action.

  • Proposals come first. 60% of restrictions since mid-2024 followed a project logged within 25 miles, compared with 48% for random towns on the same dates.
  • The lag is short. The typical gap between a nearby proposal being logged and a restriction passing is about 7 months.
  • Proposals cluster around hubs. 61% of proposed sites are within 25 miles of an operating data center (median 17 miles), and states with more data centers get more proposals (r = 0.76). That overlap is why existing hubs look like they drive backlash.
  • The spillover is the flashpoint. New campuses push past the hub’s edge into farmland and small towns, and that ring, 25 to 50 miles from a hub, is the one distance band where restrictions clearly beat chance.

Outliers. 26 restrictions are 100+ miles from any operating data center and 50+ miles from any proposal, outlined in black on the map. Kansas has 8 of them, around Wichita and the Flint Hills; others include Anchorage, Nye County (NV), Grand County (UT), northern Wisconsin, the Carolina coast and Escondido (CA).

With no project nearby, these look less like local reactions and more like contagion: towns acting preemptively as the nationwide backlash against AI and its energy and water demands spreads through news, state politics and organizing networks. Some may also reflect proposals that were never made public.

Caveat: some outliers may be caused by incomplete or inaccurate data rather than real distance. FracTracker Alliance’s list is still being built: it shows no operating data centers in Florida, Kansas or Utah and only one in Michigan, so restrictions there can look farther from data centers than they are. Proposals that were logged late, placed at the wrong location, or never logged would have the same effect.

Holding the other constant, only proposals predict restrictions

We compared all 50 states to see what best predicts how many local restrictions a state has: how many data centers it already runs, or how many new ones are being proposed. Each square shows how much restrictions go up when that number doubles. The line through it is the likely range. A square right of the dotted line means more restrictions; one sitting on the line means no effect. Explains is the share of the differences between states that each one accounts for. Proposals and operating data centers overlap: together they explain 41%, of which 27% could be credited to either, and on top of that proposals add 13% while operating data centers add under 1%.

The main model
Restrictionss ~ NegativeBinomial(μs, α)
log(μs) = β0 + β1·log2(1 + Operatings) + β2·log2(1 + Proposeds)
s
One of the 50 states. Each state is one data point.
Restrictionss
Local moratoria, bans and zoning rules the state’s counties and towns have passed (Data Center Policy Database).
μs
The number of restrictions the model expects for that state.
Operatings, Proposeds
FracTracker’s counts of operating data centers and proposed projects. Adding 1 lets states with zero stay in; log2 makes one step equal a doubling.
β0 = 0.31
Starting level: a state with no operating or proposed sites is expected to have about 1.4 restrictions (e0.31).
β1 = −0.01
Effect of operating data centers, with proposals held fixed. e−0.01 = 0.99, so −1% per doubling; range −18% to +20% (p = 0.92). This is the blue square in the main model.
β2 = 0.49
Effect of proposed projects, with operating sites held fixed. e0.49 = 1.63, so +63% per doubling; range +30% to +103% (p < 0.001). This is the orange square.
α = 0.60
Spread: the variance is μ + αμ², so states can differ more than pure chance would allow. At α = 0 this would be a plain Poisson count model.
  • Looked at alone, both seem to matter, because states with many data centers also get many new proposals.
  • Compared side by side, only proposals still count. States with the same number of proposals have about the same number of restrictions, however many data centers they already have.
  • Example: Michigan and North Carolina each have 25 proposals and about 31 restrictions, even though North Carolina has seven times as many operating data centers.
  • Why: a moratorium can only stop what isn’t built yet, so towns react to the next project.
  • Hubs still matter indirectly: they attract the proposals that spark the backlash.

Model: restrictions ~ log2(1 + operating) + log2(1 + proposed), negative binomial (the counts vary far more than a Poisson model allows: a Poisson fit leaves about 6 times the expected variance; fitted α = 0.60). In the combined model, proposed p < 0.001 and operating p = 0.92; alone, operating p = 0.004 and proposed p < 0.001. Ranges are 95% confidence intervals. Operating and proposed counts are correlated (r = 0.76) but not enough to destabilize the model (VIF 2.4). An OLS check on log(1 + restrictions) gives the same picture (proposed p = 0.004, operating p = 0.64, R² = 0.41).

    How this was measured

    Restrictions: 469 passed or enacted local policies from the Data Center Policy Database, collapsed to 432 places and placed at the town that passed them (196) or the county center (236). Data centers: 536 operating sites and 1,197 proposed, approved, suspended, expanding or cancelled projects from FracTracker Alliance. Distances are straight-line miles. Hub zones are 25-mile circles around operating sites; hub names come from grouping sites that are within 25 miles of each other. A proposal's date is when FracTracker first logged it (tracking began in April 2024), which can lag the public announcement, so "proposal first" is a conservative test. The random-town baseline uses 3,000 U.S. places with 1,000+ residents given the same dates as the restrictions. FracTracker lists no operating sites in Florida, Kansas or Utah and one in Michigan, so distances there are overstated. These are associations, not proof of cause.

    4. Data analysis and statistical methods

    Cleaning and joining

    • Each policy was attached to county FIPS codes. Town and township actions are shown on the county that contains them; a county is colored by its strongest action (ban, then moratorium, then zoning).
    • A moratorium counts as expired once its listed end date has passed. On the timeline, “expired” is judged against the date being shown, not today.
    • For distance work, the 469 policies were collapsed to 432 places and placed at the town that passed them (196) or at the county center (236).

    Distance and hubs (page 3)

    • Straight-line (haversine) miles from each restriction to the nearest operating data center, grouped into four bands: under 25, 25–50, 50–100 and 100+ miles.
    • Hubs are built by single-linkage clustering: operating sites are chained together when each is within 25 miles of another site in the group. A big hub has 12 or more sites; eight qualify.
    • “Proposal first”: a restriction counts as following a proposal if FracTracker logged a project within 25 miles before the restriction’s date. Result: 60% of restrictions since mid-2024, with a median gap of about 7 months.
    • Random-town baseline: the same test run on random GeoNames towns given the same dates gives 48%. The gap between 60% and 48% is the signal; the 48% is how often it would happen by geography alone.

    Comparing states

    • Match score is a Spearman rank correlation across the 50 states: 0.54 for operating data centers vs. restrictions, 0.67 for proposals vs. restrictions. Both are very unlikely to be chance.
    • Trend lines on the scatter plots are least-squares fits of log(1 + restrictions) on log(1 + count). The “explains” figure is that fit’s R².
    • Main model: a negative binomial regression of each state’s restriction count on log2(1 + operating) and log2(1 + proposed), so each coefficient reads as the change per doubling. Negative binomial instead of Poisson because the counts vary about six times more than Poisson allows (fitted α = 0.60). Holding the other constant, proposals: +63% restrictions per doubling (p < 0.001); operating: −1% (p = 0.92).
    • Checks: the two predictors are correlated (r = 0.76) but not enough to destabilize the model (variance inflation factor 2.4). An ordinary least-squares version on log counts tells the same story. Dropping Virginia and Texas, the two biggest outliers, does not change the result.
    • What it can’t say: these are associations among 50 states, not proof of cause.

    6. Why counting, measuring and defining data centers is hard

    • What counts as a data center? The same word covers a telecom closet, a colocation building, a cloud campus, a crypto-mining warehouse and a multi-gigawatt AI campus. Lists differ mostly in where they draw that line.
    • One project, many names. Developers often file under shell LLCs and nondisclosure agreements, and a single campus can appear as several parcels, phases or buildings. Deduplicating is judgment, not arithmetic.
    • Which measure of size? Megawatts of power, square feet, acres and dollars all get reported, rarely together, and announced megawatts are often what a developer hopes to get from the utility, not what is built.
    • Status moves fast. A project can be rumored, proposed, approved, paused, sued, cancelled and revived within a year. Any count is a snapshot, and trackers update at different speeds.
    • Policy is a moving target too. Moratoria expire or get extended, zoning rules get rewritten, and state bills can preempt local action. A “restriction” today may be lifted next month.
    • The data is political. Industry groups, utilities, advocates and local officials each have reasons to make the numbers look bigger or smaller. Trackers built by advocacy organizations may record pushback more carefully than quiet approvals. We used the sources as they are, named them on every page, and avoided claims that depend on precise totals.
